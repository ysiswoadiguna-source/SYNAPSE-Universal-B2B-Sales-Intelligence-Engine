# Domain notes

Read the section for the customer's industry before quoting or qualifying. Each lists what to collect before an offer, what the buyer usually weighs, and where deals in that domain typically stall. These are prompts for questions, not facts about any customer — anything not supplied by the user or the customer stays a blank.

## 1. Detecting the domain
Use the customer's vocabulary: units, document names, and who is in the thread. If two domains fit (for example, equipment sold into a construction project), use the domain of what is being sold for the checklist and the domain of the buyer for the decision process. If unsure, use section 9 and ask one clarifying question.

## 2. Lifting equipment
**Collect before quoting:** rated capacity · lifting height (confirm whether the figure given is lift height or chain/rope length) · span and travel length for cranes · duty class or usage pattern (lifts per hour, shifts, typical load) · power supply (voltage, phase, frequency) and control voltage · lifting and travel speeds, single or dual · control type (pendant, remote) · suspension or trolley type and the beam or runway dimensions · headroom and building constraints · environment (outdoor, dust, heat, corrosive, hazardous area) · for replacements, the existing brand, model, dimensions, and a nameplate photo · installation, commissioning, load test, and documentation scope · inspection or certification requirements at the customer's site (ask which apply; do not assume) · verified lead time.

**Buyers weigh:** safety and compliance, downtime risk, compatibility with the existing structure, local service and spare parts, total cost over the equipment's life.

**Typical stalls:** capacity given without duty; a hoist requested for a beam it does not fit; price comparison between a complete set and a bare unit; inspection or documentation requirements discovered after the offer.

**Never downplay** protection and safety devices: emergency stop, phase protection, brakes, limit switches, overload protection. If a customer asks to omit or bypass one to save cost, decline and say why.

## 3. Industrial equipment and spare parts
**Collect:** part number or nameplate, machine brand and model, quantity, condition of the current part and reason for replacement, required date, original or equivalent acceptable, any drawing or sample.

**Buyers weigh:** exact fit, downtime cost against lead time, original versus equivalent, warranty, availability of the next replacement.

**Typical stalls:** part identified by description only; equivalents offered without stating compatibility consequences; urgent requests with no one able to confirm the spec.

## 4. Engineering, construction, and projects
**Collect:** project name and owner, the customer's role (owner, main contractor, subcontractor, consultant), specification and drawings, schedule and the milestone this item serves, site conditions and access, applicable standards, approval and submittal process, payment structure the project uses.

**Buyers weigh:** specification compliance, schedule reliability, documentation, safety record, lifecycle cost, the consequence of late delivery.

**Typical stalls:** quoting to a bidder before the project is awarded; the specification written around another brand; long approval chains; payment terms tied to the customer's own collections.

## 5. Software and SaaS
**Collect:** the job the software must do and who does it today, number and type of users, systems it must integrate with, data and security requirements, who owns implementation, current tool and contract end date, how success will be measured.

**Buyers weigh:** fit to the use case, integration effort, security and data handling, implementation risk, total cost over the term, vendor stability, the cost of switching.

**Typical stalls:** a pilot with no success criteria; security or legal review starting late; a champion without budget; the incumbent contract renewing by default.

## 6. Professional services
**Collect:** the outcome wanted and why now, scope boundaries, deliverables and their acceptance criteria, timeline, who on the client side provides input and approval, how change requests are handled, the budget basis (fixed, time-based, retainer).

**Buyers weigh:** the people who will actually do the work, relevant experience, clarity of scope, governance, risk of overrun.

**Typical stalls:** scope described as an outcome with no boundaries; free consulting during the sales process; decision-makers who have not met the delivery team.

## 7. Distribution and trading
**Collect:** item and specification, quantity and frequency (one-off or recurring), delivery point, required date, price basis, payment terms requested, whether the buyer is the end user or reselling.

**Buyers weigh:** price, availability, reliability of supply, payment terms, minimum order.

**Typical stalls:** price collection with no order behind it; credit terms requested before a first transaction; margin eroded by matching every competing offer.

## 8. Financial products and services
**Collect:** the need (financing, risk cover, settlement, liquidity), amounts and tenor as the customer states them, who approves on their side, their regulatory or internal-policy constraints, required documents.

**Buyers weigh:** risk, cost, compliance, speed of approval and settlement, the relationship.

**Typical stalls:** documentation gaps; approval layers; terms that depend on assessments the salesperson cannot promise.

**Caution:** never state rates, approvals, or eligibility that the user has not confirmed, and never present a product as suitable for the customer's situation — that judgment belongs to the user's licensed colleagues and process.

## 9. General B2B fallback
**Collect:** what problem this solves, who uses it, quantity or scale, when it is needed and why then, who decides and how, what they use today.

**Buyers weigh:** whether it works, whether the supplier is reliable, total cost, and the risk of changing.

Use the seven qualifiers in `qualification.md` as the checklist.
